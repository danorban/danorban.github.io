DAN ORBAN

About Me

About the Picture Above

It is beautiful isn’t it? A loving family beside a beautiful lake, enjoying the sun, and peace… If you look closer, you can see the uncertainty and sleeplessness in our eyes. This picture was taken on May 29th, 2020, three days after the death of George Floyd and the morning after the 3rd precinct burned down. The National Guard was about to be deployed in Minneapolis and we did not know what would happen. I was there when my city burned and we learned what it was like to be alone without protection.


We had sent our kids away to the suburbs, just like the Pevensie kids in the Narnia series during the London bombings. My wife and I, however, stayed in East Phillips throughout the protests to watch our neighbors' houses and pets. Several of our neighbors left the city, uncertain of the future. My wife and I felt called to protect whatever we could, despite the potential cost. That afternoon, all I could think of was my kids. The escalation of unrest became intense. I wanted to see my kids at least one more time in case we did not survive the night. We took them on a beautiful walk near where they were staying. I treasured every moment with these little ones as if it were my last. They were innocent and precious to us, irreplaceable creatures of God's grace. I did not want to let them go.


As we said goodbye to our kids. I thought to myself, "I may never see them again." We took a deep breath with tears and returned to the city before the 8pm curfew. We took a walk down the street after removing all the rocks from our yard and turning on every light. I held my wife’s hand while other families and couples were also walking quietly and solemnly. The message was clear: “Don’t go out tonight.”


Finally, the curfew hit. We took a deep breath. In the light all was well, but in the darkness we struggled with fear, anxiety, stress, and sleeplessness. Sirens and helicopters constantly reminded us of the war outside. Alone in the house, we shared glances with each other and smiled. “Well, if this is it...” I sighed resigned to whatever our fate was. I took her hand and asked, "May I have this dance?" I don’t know why she married me, but it is a joy to be with her even in the face of fear and uncertainty.

My Story

I am a Brat

Yes, it is true. The word “Brat” best describes me. I remember walking through the streets of San Francisco watching the Blue Angels perform during Fleet Week when I finally realized the truth. As the planes roared past, I thought about my dad, the pilot, and my mother, who raised us across continents. You see, we called ourselves Military Brats, that strange breed of Third Culture Kid who would fight for truth regardless of the cost. We moved so often that home was not our home. We continually adapted to new norms and customs. We are known as the “culturally homeless”. As kids, we embodied the third culture, forced to live between traditions. We belonged to none and to many.

Every transition made us start over, adapt, make new friends, and discover how to thrive in a new environment. Imagine the trauma of losing your friends every three years. I never saw most of my friends again. Life was full of loss, but we survived and we thrived. The mission and purpose was bigger than ourselves. We existed to make the world a better, safer, and kinder place. We succeeded through difficult challenges and loneliness, for the good in this world was worth fighting for. As humans we can overcome, forgive, and love each other. We were made to do what is right, and we have the freedom and responsibility to do what is right. Yes, we were called to do our best, to do our duty, for God and our country. I am a brat.

The Homeless Years

When I left the Bay Area in 2009, I took a long bike trip, unsure of the future. I found a friend and a foe in Oregon. It's name was the Rogue River, whose waters reflected a wild lonely traveler. We were one and the same, traversing up through the forested roads. It reminded me of my other bike trips along the Rhine River in Germany and beside the windy Andulicia coast in Spain years before. The goal of the Europe trip was to find a wife. I searched for about 6 months across at least 15 countries, but alas, even the greatest of quests fail. That night, after parting ways with the Rogue, my worthy adversary, I slept on the side of an ancient volcano. The fog had forced me to pitch my tent on top of sharp rocks, and I wondered at the meaning of the adventure. That year I slept in many different places, even after I finished my bike trip.

Yes, I am no stranger to sleeping in tents, under bridges, in ditches, on park benches downtown, and on fishing docks. And there are other places to rest your head. For example, when I was in Spain I accidentally slept in a Nudist colony. In my broken Spanish, I thought they said “No-turist” instead of “Naturalist”. I simply had nowhere else to stay, but I found out my source of confusion the next day in the morning heat. I used my parent’s address to book the stay. Imagine my mother’s surprise when she opened a magazine addressed to me that was full of naked people asking, “When are you coming back?” I’m getting ahead of myself, but one summer there was no housing available for my family in New Mexico. To survive, my wife, daughter, and I lived in a tent all summer long. Even the dog joined us. We stayed among the Native dwellings, immersed beside the ancient cultures. Oh, the stories I could tell…

I know first hand that there is a sense of adventure and freedom living in a tent, experiencing life without a job. However, there are still rules to live by. I followed “Leave no Trace” principles, which means picking up after ourselves, protecting our environment, and keeping the world beautiful. When people trash the land, they do not respect it. They violate the very environment that brings life. How can those who care about our planet allow such dishonor? People have no right to destroy the soil we live on. Drugs also have no place in an adventure. They destroy our humanity and purpose, leading to sickness, death, and despair. Finally, we must love and respect each other. We should love our neighbors as ourselves. Is it loving to steal, kill, or destroy? Should we not treat women with utmost respect and dignity? Should we not protect children from evil with every breath we breathe? If we follow these rules, living in tents can perhaps work.

My adventures were dangerous and full of risk, but they were fun and led to great stories. They made me stronger, removing fear and giving me purpose and a future. These are the adventures worth having!

Affordable Housing

Although I moved to Minneapolis to play hockey, my main reason for staying was because housing was cheap. I lived in a small room with four other guys. I slept on the bottom bunk of one of the beds. This was in Eliot Park near downtown. We only used the room for sleeping. Fortunately, I did not have many possessions, so I did not take up much space. We would often spend time together eating meals, debating theological and philosophical ideas, and playing board games. Those days were wonderful and healing despite the blistering cold that made my face want to fall off. I moved to Minneapolis the week the Metrodome imploded from snow. I just figured that the Metrodome imploded every year.

I still wanted to find a wife, so I took up ballroom dancing, competing in competitions and doing solo routines. This worked! At the Tapestry, I met my wife contra dancing. Neither of us had a stable job at that time, but I fell in love with her because she cared for the “least of these”, those who were lonely, disabled, elderly, and homeless. I was especially impressed at how she loved and cared for the older generation. People forget about our elders and the wisdom they possess, hidden treasures lost to lonely homes and facilities. My beautiful wife cared for others more than she cared for herself.

We were married at Powderhorn Park, inviting everyone we knew and their friends. It was an open invitation to celebrate! People from all walks of life were there. Some we knew, and some we didn’t. Neither of us had a car at the time. After the wedding, we rode our bikes, wedding dress and all, to Ventura Village, where we moved into a cheap studio apartment.

Soon after, we found a house in East Phillips that needed a ton of work. We got a great deal because it was a foreclosure and we were first time home buyers. Even today, the mortgage is practically the same as it was 10 years ago, not fluctuating with the market. It was the best investment we could make as a young family seeking stability and belonging. People often call us asking to buy our house. We always tell them “no”. They are often curious what we plan to use it for. I usually say, “It’s our home! We live here.”

The Trouble with Transportation

I love the environment and its beauty! As humans, we are called to cultivate and cherish our wonderful, surprising, and glorious world. The diversity of life and its mystery continue to beckon us to explore. We must go further up and further in, curiosity being our guide. When I lived in the SOMA and Mission districts of San Francisco, I decided to live without a car for several reasons. First, I thought it might be fun and challenging. Second, I wanted to save money. Third, I wanted to be above reproach. If I were going to tell people that I cared about the creation, I thought that my life should be at least consistent with my message. I also wanted to free myself from the addiction of driving.

Living without a car was difficult, but also rewarding. I used the bus and train for the commute to work in Palo Alto and San Jose. I biked everywhere! I would often take Bart out to Mount Diablo, biking up to the top. I got pulled over for biking on the freeway by accident. I sometimes got stuck outside in the rain and cold overnight. Most people, even in the Bay Area, did not understand my carless lifestyle. A coworker’s car once broke down, and she had to ride the bus to work. She said, “I now understand how people in poverty live. They have no time for anything else except to wait at bus stops all day long.” There is some truth to her statement. Often, I would have to get home late, or skip a friend’s party. One simple event might take all day to get to. Being without a car can be tough, unless you live near your work and social community. Good weather is also a must!

As mentioned before, I left the Bay Area on a bike to find a new home. After a year or so of traveling, I ended up in Minnesota, still without a car. I actually thought the transportation system in Minneapolis and the bike paths far surpassed that of California. Buses and the light rail trains were fairly clean. I loved the frequency of the buses! I found it easy and intuitive to get around. I could walk to the grocery store, to church, and to work. Winter, of course, was tough, but doable. I could bike anywhere in the Twin Cities in the summer. As a single person in Minnesota, living without a car can be fantastic.

After I got married, life changed drastically. My wife worked in Roseville and we lived in South Minneapolis. She would have to bike 9 miles most days, which took away much of our newlywed time. She soon became pregnant with our first child and it got cold. Biking was no longer a possibility. I started to worry about her walking and slipping on the ice to catch the bus. At the urging of friends and family, we got a car to help us. Neither of us really wanted one, but it was practical for the season.

It quickly became expensive to maintain the car on our limited income, but by God’s grace, we were able to make payments, pay for gas, and keep it running. I was proud of myself for teaching my wife to drive a stick shift.

Several years later, the jeep we inherited from our parents finally gave up the ghost. It simply stopped moving. It seemed like we were constantly bringing it into the shop for repairs costing thousands of dollars. We would not be able to easily afford this repair, so we decided to return to our roots and go carless again. By now we were a family of four. We lived in East Phillips near grocery stores and school. My wife worked from home and at the Mall of America. I worked at the University of Minnesota and in Phillips. Our friends were in the neighborhood. Everything was close by!

It was fun to live without a car again. It felt good to teach our kids how to ride the bus and train. We biked everywhere again, traveling to different nearby parks and lakes using the greenway and bike paths. We started saving money, eating less, and getting exercise. There are advantages to being carless, but it can also be very difficult for a family, especially in the winter. Again, to get anywhere further than our local community would be challenging and time consuming. Walking with a three year old in the snow and ice is insane. It’s hard to bring strollers on the buses. Buses and trains are occasionally unsafe and unsanitary venues for kids. Planning multiple events across town required special logistical minds. In other words, being carless is not always easy or practical. In fact, we currently have a car, but we like to bus and bike when possible. Let’s just say that I am well qualified to relate to the transportation needs for everyone in our city, both for drivers and the carless.

Sustainable Living

My first professional job was in Round Rock, TX working for Dell Inc. I lived in Austin, TX at the time near Zilker Park and the Barton Creek Greenbelt. I was a computer programmer building websites. If you do a search for some software or peripheral on Dell's website, you are probably touching something that I historically had a hand in. Yes, I know technology, and I know it well. I am the world expert on understanding and visualizing parameter spaces in light of large, high- dimensional, and unstructured datasets. In other words, “I am the expert on knowing how little I know about how much I can know.” Please see my dissertation for additional details. My wife keeps me humble by describing my research as, “Fun with colorful polka dots.”

Throughout my life, however, I have endeavored to raise up the next generation as an educator. I’ve taught K-12 youth, college students, and industry professionals. A troubling trend has appeared on my radar. We are addicted to technology and the power needed to maintain our addictions. Kids can no longer concentrate at school because they would rather mindlessly scroll or watch videos. Their brains are instead filled with repetition and hurtful, unloving speech on social media. Their friends are online instead of on the playground. Young kids spend their time on video games when days are beautiful outside.

Adults are not immune to the tech catastrophe that has endangered the very structure of civilization. Even I feel compelled to answer a text over playing trains with my son. Parents, myself included, are endlessly scrolling through news articles or buying items online instead of going to local stores. We are destroying the social links in our society as well as our economy.

My wife and I needed a break from the world we live in. In fact, our whole family needed a break from technology. In typical Orban fashion, we dove head first into a new adventure. In the summer of 2023, we lived on an old Amish Hobby farm in Southeastern Minnesota. They call the region the Driftless because the glaciers did not flatten the limestone. There are thousands of sinkholes in the region leading to diverse cave systems. In any case, we decided to try our hands at farming from the ground up (pun intended).

Since it was an old Amish farm, the house had no electricity or plumbing. We decided to try and limit the amount of energy needed to run the farm. The first task was to pump water from the well. Surprisingly, although the Amish in the region do not use electricity, they are allowed to use gas powered motors. We adopted this as a pragmatic approach to getting water. I secured a motor to a platform and adjusted a pulley system to pump water into a 1500 gallon cistern. I’m proud of this accomplishment because at least two seasoned farmers said that the setup was “slick”. A friend from the city came down to help me fix the plumbing so the indoor hand pumps could pull water from the cistern indoors. We can pump hundreds of gallons of water with very little gas needed. My wife wants to someday hook up a bicycle to the pulley system so we can use calories instead of gas, but that is another project.

The Amish used to use kerosene for lighting at night. We personally thought we could do better here, so we bought a bunch of solar powered lanterns that we would charge on the laundry lines during the day. The lights would last all night on the low setting, powered by the sun. I jumped in head first into solar powered energy. A friend of mine and I built a custom solar system using a 100 watt solar panel, a bluetooth enabled solar charger, and a 20 amp hour 12V battery. This system simply ran a fan for our composting toilet and a more powerful kitchen light during the night. It would also charge our phones through a USB adapter. I started creating simple 12V low power solutions around the house for fun.

Soon, I started charging rechargeable AA and AAA batteries that were used for additional motion powered lights and the toilet fan. I also got a simple electric water pump working that could eventually provide water to an elevated tank for pressure using gravity. Eventually, we added 2 more 100 watt solar panels and a 400 watt inverter so we could charge power tools. We could now support battery powered electrical tools including a chainsaw and a weedwacker. The possibilities are endless here to charge lawn mowers, drills, lights, fans, and pumps. We are hoping someday to upgrade the inverter to support machines that make ice. It is ironic that the best time to make ice is midday when the sun is the hottest. In general, the solar powered system can support all the current electrical needs in the house simply using the sun!

When we first started farming, we discovered that there was no adequate sewage system. The accepted standard in the country is to dig a septic system. Although there was a septic for gray water, it would not handle sewage. A simple temporary solution that saved $20,000, was to buy a modern composting toilet. Being from the city, this seemed like it was going to be weird and awkward. Imagine not flushing a toilet all summer, but rather separating solids and liquids. As a city person, it is hard to think about. Surprisingly, the composting toilet was amazing and met our needs completely! It did not smell, was easy to clean, and felt good from an environmental point of view. Using composting material and a simple solar powered electric fan, everything took care of itself. There was no waste! Water was not needed, and the solids from the compost will eventually be used to provide fertilizer to trees and plants in the pasture.

For cooking and heating we used wood burning stoves. Fortunately we could cut wood using the solar powered chainsaw. My wife also has a solar powered oven that focuses sunlight on a glass container. She occasionally uses it for soup, potatoes, and egg bake. We had many outside bonfires to cook brats and smores.

Halfway through the summer, my wife decided that she wanted goats to help weed and fertilize a future garden. We set up a solar powered electric fence for goats to go to work. As they devoured the vegetation, we rotated them throughout the plot. Each time they moved, we would plow the field, remove weeds, and plant a cover crop. My wife also started planting vegetables and fruit trees. The harvest at the end of the summer was rewarding. I think the goats were happy too!

We had many more projects that summer, but we learned how to save energy in creative efficient ways. Of course, there is more work left to be done. This winter, we plan to freeze buckets of ice from the well to cool an insulated room in the basement during the summer. This will keep food fresh and cold all year long without electricity. Saving energy and being sustainable means living very cheap with free energy from the sun. My wife wants to build similar sustainable living environments in the city, perfectly suitable for those who need housing. They would be affordable, energy efficient, and beneficial to the environment. This is especially true for solar powered and composting solutions. The trick is to figure out how it can scale to multi-family and high density housing, but it seems like it could work, especially when combined with shared community gardens.

A Change in Culture

Sometimes it is good to do something completely different. We are often blind to the planks that cover our eyes when we live in a place. Rarely do we ask, “Is this how it should be?” Instead, we say, “I guess this is how it is, so let’s adapt.” When we came back from the farm, we saw the exact opposite of life. People were existing in poverty, pain, and sickness. Tales of death in our alley and fires across the street plagued our souls. The people in the encampments told me stories of inadequate treatment from the city and of violent stabbings (the word “butchering” was actually used) from unhealthy people on drugs. Kids were not allowed outside anymore to play in their backyards. My neighbors were afraid to throw their trash away. I called the city and the city council for help, but no one would answer or return my calls. People were okay with the theft, death, and destruction.

I asked a neighbor what they thought and they said that they understood why it had to be that way. I couldn’t understand why people would be okay with this! Is this the new normal? Why were people in encampments not being cared for? Why was no one looking after the residents? I wondered if I would have said the same if I had not been removed for the summer. It felt like a deadly lullaby, putting us to sleep so that our city could be devoured by some evil force.

My wife and I started noticing that businesses across the city were gone, boarded up, empty, and sad. In the places of great economic growth and social strength, stood tombs of what could have been. Crime had become normal. A stolen car was nothing. Hateful yelling and disrespectful words filled our ears. Our kids were sad at the trash on the street and the smell of feces in our garbage. The trash bin had the words “R.I.P Kylee” written on it. I called the cops to check to see if it had a body in it, but they never came to my knowledge.

What had happened to our lovely home? Why was no one doing anything to make this place beautiful? Why can my kids no longer play with the neighbor kids in the backyard? Kids with guns are pointing them at other kids. People are getting robbed while walking their dogs. Children are getting kidnapped and women raped. How can we sit by with a clear conscience and allow this to happen?

We are called to make this world a better place, a more beautiful place, and a loving place. We are called to love our neighbor as ourselves, to care for the least of these, to love our enemies, and to overcome evil with good. This isn’t about what I want, but what needs to be done. I cannot sit back and watch my city be destroyed by people who won’t do anything. It is our responsibility to make things right and good and loving. I am simply taking up the role of truth in a democracy.

I’m not here for myself, but for all the other people who feel powerless and forgotten in our city. Let’s take back our neighborhoods for what is true, good, and right. Let’s treat all people with respect and dignity. Let’s forget about our political biases, our petty arguments, our factions, and even our very own lives to do good things together. This world is growing darker, but people have the amazing power and authority to bring light and life to a dead and dying city. It is our glorious mandate. Join me in the fight for Ward 9, the very place where the modern world was broken. Let’s create a garden city, working towards the one we miss, long for, and dream of… Let’s overcome evil with good.
